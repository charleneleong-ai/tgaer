# Swap the explorer for the Duck, then tune

The rules of the ARC Prize 2026 ARC-AGI-3 Kaggle competition have not materially changed since early summer. The limits are a **9-hour** runtime (raised from 6 h on 2026-05-07, when H100s were swapped for a single **RTX PRO 6000, 96 GB**), **internet off**, open weights allowed with no size cap, **1 submission per day**, **2 final selections**, and a squared action-efficiency metric scored on **110 unseen games** that are never rerun at the end. No rule change was announced in late August or September ([Kaggle Code Requirements](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/overview/code-requirements); [Update on Code Requirements](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/discussion/697944)). The leaderboard, however, has moved a great deal. The Milestone-1 winner scored **1.21** in June. On 2026-09-30 the leader, Tufa Labs, sits at **45.33**, #10 at 13.40, and the median at 0.32 across 3,492 teams ([Kaggle leaderboard](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/leaderboard)). Almost all of that rise came from one architecture: a local LLM that writes and runs Python in a REPL against the live game (Tufa's "Duck"), with each jump coming from a stronger Qwen model and faster serving rather than from new harness machinery. Team tgaer's model-free explorer scores publicScore **0.13–0.14** across twelve submissions, below the 25th percentile. The team already has the exact model the Duck won Milestone 1 with (Qwen3.6-27B-FP8), attached to its kernel and unused. The audit's verdict is blunt: the team is not short of mechanisms. It is running the architecture that the whole top of the board abandoned. The first-priority move, ahead of any explorer change, is to fork the public Duck/TAAF baseline with a current Qwen serving stack and spend the remaining ~33 daily slots improving that.

**Compute and runtime.** Kaggle's settings object lists `maxGpuRuntimeMinutes: 540`. The "<12 hours" figure that circulates, and that the team's notes carried as a possibility, comes from ARC Prize's separate *verified-leaderboard* policy. That policy also permits internet ([arcprize.org/policy](https://arcprize.org/policy)). Greg Kamradt confirmed that it does not apply to this competition. The team's kernel assumes **7.5 h** (`KERNEL_BUDGET_H`, `_RUN_DEADLINE`). No official source supports that number, so the kernel leaves about **1.5 h (17%) of the budget unused**. The machine is a GCP `g4-standard-48`: 48 vCPU, 180 GB RAM, one RTX PRO 6000 with 96 GB GDDR7 ([GCP GPU machine types](https://cloud.google.com/compute/docs/gpus)). The organisers have also said that `/kaggle/working` has a 20 GB disk quota, container logs are capped at 10 MB, and there is a "4-core CPU allocation" ([724841](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/discussion/724841)). That last figure conflicts with the 48 vCPU spec. Anyone planning CPU-parallel work beside the LLM should log `nproc` inside a scored run rather than assume either number.

**Internet, APIs and models.** Internet is disabled during scoring, and "all RTX sessions must have internet disabled", so GPT, Claude and Gemini APIs cannot be used ([Upgraded accelerators](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/overview/upgraded-accelerators); [arcprize.org/competitions/2026](https://arcprize.org/competitions/2026)). "Freely & publicly available external data is allowed, including pre-trained models." In practice the only parameter cap is VRAM plus runtime. Participants report running GPT-OSS-120B and Qwen 3.x 27B-class models on the card ([738599](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/discussion/738599)). As of 2026-09-28 there is still "no official way" to package a large model offline; the host points people to public notebooks ([743785](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/discussion/743785)).

**Scoring.** Each completed level scores min(human/agent actions, 1) squared. Within a game, levels are weighted by index; the total is averaged across games ([Kaggle Data](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/data)). Every submission plays all **110 private games**. 55 of them feed the public leaderboard and the other 55 a hidden private score, which is fixed when the submission runs. The 25 public games are for development only and are never scored ([684852](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/discussion/684852)). The leaderboard is on a 0–100 scale truncated to two decimals, so tgaer's 0.13 means 0.13%, and differences under 0.01 cannot be seen. A community report of byte-identical agents scoring 0.20 and 0.03 has no host reply ([738762](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/discussion/738762)). This suggests stochastic LLM agents can swing widely between runs, even though the team's one deterministic explorer repeat pair reproduced exactly.

**Milestones, deadlines and open source.** Milestone #2 closes **today, 2026-09-30 23:59 UTC**, and there is no Milestone #3. Entry and team mergers close 2026-10-26. Final submissions are due **2026-11-02 23:59 UTC**, the paper track closes 2026-11-09 (arcprize.org says Nov 8), and winners are announced 2026-12-04 ([Timeline](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/overview/timeline)). Kaggle lists the milestone split as $25K/$7.5K/$5K. arcprize.org lists $25K/$10K/$2.5K, and Milestone #1 was in fact announced on the latter split ([arcprize.org](https://arcprize.org/competitions/2026/arc-agi-3); [Milestone 1 blog](https://arcprize.org/blog/arc-prize-2026-milestone-1)). Three official texts disagree on the licence. Kaggle's Rules give **CC-BY 4.0** as the winner licence and require an "open source system, open source model, and open source weights" under the OSI definition. The Prizes page says only "an open source license". arcprize.org requires self-authored code under **CC0 or MIT-0** ([Rules](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/rules)). Releasing your own code under CC0/MIT-0 with OSI-licensed dependencies satisfies all three. Qwen's Apache-2.0 weights are safer here than Gemma's custom terms, although Gemma-based entries still won Milestone 1 prizes. Two September questions remain unanswered: dependency datasets marked "License: Unknown" ([743753](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/discussion/743753)), and whether self-generated synthetic game data counts as external data ([742940](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/discussion/742940)). The newsworthy September event was a choice by a team, not a rule change: Tufa Labs said it will not open-source for Milestone 2 and will release only after the competition ends ([742801](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/discussion/742801)).

## A 37x leaderboard rise came mostly from swapping models under one harness

Two scoreboards need to be kept apart. On the **Kaggle board**, which is offline, uses open weights and is scored on hidden games, the top of the table on 2026-09-30 reads: Tufa Labs 45.33, Yi-Chia Chen 36.73, Daniel Franzen 26.55, Lord Han Solo 22.24, Tong Hui Kang 20.53. The NVIDIA grandmaster team NVARC3 is 8th at 16.07. The distribution is extremely skewed: p90 = 3.89, p75 = 2.62, median 0.32, p25 = 0.15 ([Kaggle leaderboard](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/arc-prize-2026-arc-agi-3/leaderboard)). The team's own memory has the leader at 19.40 on 2026-09-24. If that is right, the leader more than doubled in the final week before the milestone, with methods that are not in any public notebook. Nobody outside Tufa knows how 45.33 was reached.

What *is* public follows one lineage. The Milestone-1 winner, Tufa's Duck, encodes game observations as Python variables in a live REPL. It shows the grid both as an image and as text with segmentation helpers, evicts old messages so play can continue indefinitely, and ran **Qwen3.6-27B FP8** locally for a milestone score of 1.21. Tufa's own public-set estimate was 1.60 ± 0.45 over 25 games × 20 attempts, and a rerun did not reproduce the "lucky" 1.21 ([Tufa Duck notebook](https://www.kaggle.com/code/jeroencottaar/tufa-labs-duck-harness-june-30-milestone-winner); [Tufa research](https://tufalabs.ai/research/duck-harness)). Tufa's main lesson was that gains came from "multimodality and better base models, not hand-built tools"; hand-crafted tools "actually hurt the model" ([Milestone 1 blog](https://arcprize.org/blog/arc-prize-2026-milestone-1)). Second (Reki) and third (forge) were vision LLMs emitting one JSON action per step on Gemma-4-31B. forge's best configuration switched off most of its own machinery. After June, public forks kept the Duck's prompts and loop and changed what sits underneath. A Duck fork on **Qwen3.8-27B-FP8** was at about LB 9 by mid-August ([foysalemonshanto](https://www.kaggle.com/code/foysalemonshanto/lb-9-arc3-duck-v12-with-qwen-3-8-27b)). By September the most-forked stack ran **Qwen3.8-Flash-Next in NVFP4** with 3-token MTP speculative decoding, 32K context, 8 vLLM sequences, CUDA graphs and 28-way game concurrency, with the "Duck prompts, loop and policy unchanged" ([keithtyser](https://www.kaggle.com/code/keithtyser/duck-qwen3-8-flash-next-nvfp4-mtp)). Its author is #15 at 9.17. The remaining public gains came from perception and loop robustness, not new reasoning machinery. Scott Le Grand's fork adds six "agentfix" repairs: one board image per request, action results always returned, tolerant world-model parsing with no wipe on game-over, identical-snippet and no-action loop guards, HUD-band-aware change detection with animation summaries, and an executable ACTION7 ([scottlegrand](https://www.kaggle.com/code/scottlegrand/taaf-flashnext-sheetu12b-0922)). A solo competitor's biggest single gain was rendering the board at 512×512 instead of 256×256 (`MULTIMODAL_UPSCALE` 4 → 8). Every module added after that regressed ([Shih-Yu-Yeh journal](https://github.com/Shih-Yu-Yeh/arc3-dev-agi-journal)). This ranking of levers (model and throughput, then perception, then loop robustness, with hand-built tools last) is inferred from fork notes. No team has published a controlled Kaggle ablation.

The **frontier-API** world has saturated the 25 public games, and those numbers do not transfer to Kaggle. Tycho, NVIDIA AVO and Retrodict all report 99.86–100 RHAE on the public 183 levels. GPT-6 Astra reaches 99.9% under a context-preserving harness versus 62.7% under the standard one ([Tycho](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28287); [NVIDIA AVO](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-avo-reaches-100-on-arc-agi-3-demonstrating-a-frontier-level-general-purpose-architecture-for-long-horizon-autonomous-agents/); [Retrodict](https://github.com/ryanbbrown/Retrodict); [Astra](https://arcprize.org/blog/astra)). They cannot run offline, so their value here is the ideas that transfer. Retrodict tests every hypothesis against the recorded history in Python *before* spending actions, states the expected board with each action, re-invokes the model with a diff only on a mismatch, and probes with single actions until the mechanics are confirmed, after which it batches moves. Twin clears 179/183 levels, **infers the goal before any reward on 87% of levels**, and concludes that "building a usable world model is simpler than anticipated, whereas the harder problem is inferring the right goal" ([Twin](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14490)). Rodionov's ablation finds that verification helps at lower reasoning effort and stops mattering at maximum effort ([arXiv 2607.15439](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.15439)). A 27B model is firmly in the lower-effort regime, so of the three mechanisms verification is the one most likely to help it. The only in-kernel small-model paper, AERA (Qwen2.5-0.5B, explore → verify → plan), scores **0.30 on the private set**, and its no-explore baseline scores zero ([AERA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.25931)). That is about twice tgaer's public score from a model 50x smaller. None of these transfer claims has been tested with a ≤30B open model in the kernel.

## Twelve submissions, thirty levers, one architecture ceiling

The team's own records show a thorough, disciplined programme aimed at the wrong system. The shipped `ExplorerArcAgi3Agent` is a model-free, frontier-driven directed state graph over chrome-masked, field-cropped frame signatures ([`src/tgaer/agents/arc_agi3_explorer.py`](../src/tgaer/agents/arc_agi3_explorer.py)). More than 30 levers were gated against seed error bars. Local RHAE on the 25 public games rose about 3x: from 0.12% in August, through the chrome mask (+38%) and `PROBE_LIMIT=1` (+0.1959pp), to about **0.42%** ([challenge log](../docs/challenges/arc-agi-3.md)). PublicScore did not move. Twelve explorer submissions have never left {0.13, 0.14}. A 2.25x local jump (v75 → v76) scored 0.13 → 0.13, and the team's best-ever 0.17 still belongs to a v54 LLM agent that clears zero levels ([pinned-score memory](/Users/charleneleong/.claude/projects/-Users-charleneleong-Dropbox-Mac-Documents-gen-ai-orak-hackathon-tgaer/memory/project_arcagi3_public_score_is_pinned.md)). The sweeps of 2026-09-29 found 0 of 17 constant settings that gain. Every ordering, routing, prior or carry mechanism tried after 2026-09-18 was rejected, bit-identical, or inside noise. The team's own documents already call constant tuning "exhausted".

The most useful internal measurement compares the Duck's published example runs with the team's cached trajectories. The Duck clears at least one level in **23 of 25** public games; the explorer clears **7 of 25**. Where both clear level 1, the Duck needs **55–69 actions** and the explorer **383–1,708** (m0r0 67 vs 1,708; s5i5 56 vs 1,401; sp80 55 vs 458; tu93 69 vs 383). Because the metric squares the action ratio, a 6–25x action overrun keeps only about 0.2–3% of a level's value. The explorer is deeper only on lp85 and tu93. That result lines up with three structural findings. First, **selection is at chance**: the winning move is proposable 97% of the time but chosen first only 18%. Second, five games are blinded by the field crop (tr87 maps 523 boards to one signature). Third, every scoring game is efficiency-limited: 80–98% of actions on uncleared levels revisit boards already seen ([coverage memory](/Users/charleneleong/.claude/projects/-Users-charleneleong-Dropbox-Mac-Documents-gen-ai-orak-hackathon-tgaer/memory/project_arcagi3_coverage_not_selection.md); [world-model log](../docs/arc-agi3-world-model.md)). A graph explorer without a model of *which* untested action matters is close to its ceiling. The earlier literature review reached the same conclusion from theory: nearest-frontier traversal is already Θ(log n)-competitive, so the remaining headroom sits in the model layer, not the traversal layer.

The team's LLM attempts did not fail because LLMs cannot do this. The team's LLM agents were **policy** agents, and the one **programmer** test was one-shot. Qwen3.6-27B as a tool-calling policy cleared zero levels at 200 actions. The early prompt revisions that scored 0.02–0.17 were noise, because chat history overflowed `n_ctx=4096` by step 3. The LLM-codegen line (seven attempts at a frozen `policy(frame)`) produced no promotions. The one-shot "write a distance function" test found a usable gradient in only 2 of 11 games. None of these runs the loop that makes the Duck work: probe the live game from inside code, revise a written hypothesis when it is contradicted, then hand off to self-written search ([LLM-as-programmer memory](/Users/charleneleong/.claude/projects/-Users-charleneleong-Dropbox-Mac-Documents-gen-ai-orak-hackathon-tgaer/memory/project_arcagi3_llm_as_programmer.md)). The team built that loop on `feat/arc-agi3-repl-harness` (2026-08-22: 1,077 lines, 341 of them tests) and never measured it. Its one attempt to run the real Duck, on a shared A100 pod with no native FP8, reached 40–130 total tokens/s at 16 games and was stopped as "not a fair test". **The architecture that leads the board has not yet been evaluated by this team.**

## What to do in the 33 days left, in priority order

At one slot per day there are about 33 scored submissions before 2026-11-02, and kernel publishing locks on 2026-10-26. The order below puts first the steps whose evidence comes from the hidden set itself (the public leaderboard) and last the steps with only public-game or frontier-model evidence.

| # | Action | Evidence it pays | Cost / risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Submit a public Duck/TAAF fork unchanged**, using the Qwen3.8-Flash-Next NVFP4 + MTP serving stack, to establish a hidden-set baseline | Forks of this exact stack sit at LB ~9 (#15), about 65x the current 0.14; Qwen3.6-27B alone won 1.21 in June | 1 slot. NVFP4 needs Blackwell, so dev iteration has to use Kaggle RTX sessions (which burn quota fast), not the A100 pod. Confirm the weight licence is OSI-compatible |
| 2 | **Raise the wall-clock budget from 7.5 h to about 8.5 h** | Official limit is 540 min | A config change. Keep a margin for vLLM install (10–15 min) and weight load |
| 3 | **Fold in the published perception and loop fixes one at a time**: 512px render, animation-frame handling, HUD-aware change detection, loop guards, no world-model wipe on game-over | Each is the largest single gain in a public fork or journal; add-ons beyond these regressed | 1 slot each. Stop any fix whose score falls by ≥0.5 on the public LB |
| 4 | **Add Retrodict-style retrodiction to the prompt**: check a hypothesis against the logged transitions in the REPL before acting, and state the expected board with each batched plan | Verification matters most at low reasoning effort, which is the 27B regime | Prompt-only. Frontier evidence only, untested at 27B |
| 5 | **Explicit goal-inference step** once the dynamics are probed (Twin), plus prompt licence to use reset as an experiment | Goal inference named as the harder bottleneck; humans use about 12.5% resets as experiments ([AutumnBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.19788v1)) | Prompt-only, frontier evidence |
| 6 | **Offer the explorer's machinery as *optional* REPL helpers**: state-hash graph, BFS to frontier, inert-action detection, chrome mask | These are the team's proven assets. Reki's dead-signature pruning was part of a 2nd-place system | The Duck found forced tools *hurt*. Expose them, don't mandate them, and gate on the public LB |
| 7 | **Choose the two final submissions from different run families**, and resubmit the leading candidate once to estimate its variance | Tufa could not reproduce its own 1.21; byte-identical LLM agents reportedly scored 0.20 vs 0.03 | Private scores are frozen at run time, so a lucky public draw is the main selection risk |
| 8 | **Stop spending slots on explorer constants**, value models, effect prediction and LoRA test-time training | Explorer public score pinned across 12 submissions; effect-prediction ceiling +0.0065pp; aTTT gives about +5 points at 1.9x wall-clock and "stabilizes existing competence" rather than creating it ([aTTT](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.03441v1)) | Zero cost to drop |

Three caveats bound this plan. First, the lever ranking in rows 1–3 comes from fork descriptions and leaderboard titles, not from ablations. It could be that the Duck prompt, not the model upgrade, carries most of the gain; the first two submissions should be designed to tell the two apart. Second, the team's own finding that the 25-game local bench does not predict the public leaderboard was measured on a deterministic explorer. For a stochastic LLM agent the public leaderboard itself is noisy. Tufa's ±0.45 local spread on a mean of 1.6 is a coefficient of variation near 28%. Single-slot A/B verdicts need a margin larger than that. Third, no public method explains the leader's 45.33, and Tufa has said it will publish only after the deadline. Reaching the #50 band (5.55) is plausible from public material; reaching the top 10 (13.40 and up) probably requires a finding nobody has published.

## Conclusion

The audit changes the question the team has been asking. For two months the working hypothesis was that the 25-game bench was a poor proxy and that the right explorer mechanism would eventually show up on the leaderboard. The broader data say the proxy problem was real but not what held the score down. On Kaggle's hidden games, every entry above the 90th percentile is an LLM writing code in a REPL, and the ranking inside that group tracks the model and its serving throughput more closely than any harness feature. The explorer programme was rigorous and still produced a useful asset: a fast, GPU-free state graph with a measured inertness detector and chrome mask. Its best use now is as an optional tool inside someone else's winning loop, not as the policy itself.

The earlier literature review found that dev-set gains transfer bimodally and that nobody publishes a public-to-private correlation. That now has a concrete consequence: the only reliable gate left is the public leaderboard, a genuine 55-game held-out set, and the team has about 33 reads of it. Those reads carry more information when spent on large architectural steps whose expected effect (tens of times the current score) dwarfs the noise than on sub-0.05pp constant nudges that the leaderboard cannot even display.
